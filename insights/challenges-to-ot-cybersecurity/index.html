Executive summary

Critical infrastructure is now a standing target. The question is no longer whether a network will be probed, but whether a probe can ever become a path.

In modern industrial and critical infrastructure environments, cyber threats are constant and evolving. Any system — directly or indirectly — can become a point of vulnerability. Power grids, substations, energy plants, dams and industrial control systems are increasingly targeted by ransomware, intrusion attempts and data exfiltration campaigns.

To address this, the unidirectional data diode provides a hardware-enforced, air-gapped solution that ensures secure, one-way data transfer between isolated networks — without exposing internal systems to any form of return communication or logical interaction.

Unlike firewalls or proxies that rely on configurable software, the diode operates through fixed, FPGA-driven logic. It allows only authorized protocols and file types while blocking all others at the hardware level — delivering deterministic, tamper-proof data flow with zero exposure.

“Data leaves. Nothing returns. The return path is not blocked by a rule that can be changed — it does not physically exist.”

Introduction

Operational Technology was built for safety, reliability and uptime. It was never built to be attacked.

Operational Technology (OT) encompasses the hardware and software systems that directly monitor and control physical processes within critical infrastructure. Unlike traditional IT systems focused on data, OT networks are built for safety, reliability and continuous operation across sectors such as power, energy, oil and gas, manufacturing, water treatment and transportation.

Historically these environments were isolated and not designed with modern cybersecurity in mind. Most systems are decades old, with legacy components that lack security features. The escalating convergence of IT and OT networks — driven by digitization and the Industrial Internet of Things — has exposed this entire ecosystem to a new and dangerous wave of threats.

In this converged landscape, a digital intrusion can have devastating real-world consequences, from catastrophic physical damage and service disruption to endangering human lives. Vulnerable legacy systems and the demand for 24/7 uptime create a fragile environment where a specialized, proactive approach is no longer optional.

Real-world OT exploits

  • Ukraine power grid, 2015. Attackers used spear-phishing and BlackEnergy malware to shut down power for 230,000 people.
  • Stuxnet. A worm that damaged Iranian centrifuges by manipulating PLCs through USB-based infection.
  • Colonial Pipeline, 2021. Ransomware actors reached the IT network via a compromised VPN. OT was not directly breached, yet interdependency forced an operational shutdown and major fuel disruption across the U.S. East Coast.

Challenges

Vulnerabilities, bugs and exploit potential in the current landscape.

Common vulnerabilities in OT environments

  • Legacy systems. Most OT devices run outdated software and hardware with limited or no vendor support, and are often incompatible with modern security controls.
  • Lack of network segmentation. OT networks frequently run flat architectures. Once inside, an attacker moves laterally across critical devices.
  • Inadequate authentication. Devices often rely on weak credentials, or none at all, allowing easy unauthorized access.
  • Insecure protocols. Modbus, OPC and their peers were designed without encryption or integrity checks, exposing data to tampering and interception.
  • Limited monitoring. Many OT networks lack real-time visibility, making anomaly and intrusion detection difficult.
  • Insider threats. Personnel with physical or digital access can cause disruption, intentionally or otherwise.
  • IT/OT convergence. Each new connection introduces fresh vulnerability, including ransomware spread and remote exploitation.
  • Supply chain risk. Compromised hardware or firmware can enter OT environments undetected.
  • Physical security gaps. Many OT systems sit in remote or physically insecure locations.

Why Terafence

How the Terafence Secure Gateway works: unidirectional, secure, controlled transfer.

The Terafence hardware-based data diode enforces one-way communication using immutable hardware logic. Data can only flow from the transmitting side — the OT environment — to the receiving side, eliminating all reverse-flow possibilities.

The transmitting network initiates non-persistent sessions: it can send, but can never receive in return. The receiving side has no physical or logical channel to communicate back, making bidirectional exploitation or control impossible.

The diode is built on FPGA technology and operates solely at OSI layers 1 and 2, providing pure physical-layer control over transmission. It carries no IP or MAC address in the FPGA data-path core, so it cannot be scanned, pinged or attacked in conventional ways. The core requires no software and does not interact with any other device on the network.

Risks eliminated

  • No reverse channel. One-way communication is guaranteed physically. There is no backdoor and no software agent to exploit.
  • Layer 1 and 2 security. Hardware-enforced control below the network layer leaves zero room for IP-based attack.
  • No attack surface. The FPGA core on the data path has no IP or MAC address.
  • Air-gap equivalence. Replicates an air-gapped network while still enabling monitoring, telemetry and secure patch distribution.
  • Supply chain security. No internal firmware or OS in the core means no hidden software vulnerability to exploit.
